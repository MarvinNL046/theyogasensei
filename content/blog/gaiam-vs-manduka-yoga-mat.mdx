Gaiam versus Manduka is a money question wearing a gear question's clothes. Both brands sell a 6mm PVC mat. Both promise a lifetime guarantee. One costs several times the other.

The gap is not cushion. It is weight, length, how long the surface holds up, and what those two guarantees actually cover when you read them.

A note on what this is. I have not practised on either mat. Our [Gaiam Premium review](/reviews/gaiam) and our [Manduka PRO review](/reviews/manduka-pro) are both research-and-specs reviews. Every figure below comes from Gaiam's own pages, Manduka's own pages, or OutdoorGearLab's testing, and I say which each time.

## The short answer

Buy the **Gaiam Premium 6mm** if you are new, on a budget, or not yet sure yoga will stick. It is cushioned, light, and cheap enough that quitting costs you almost nothing.

Buy the **Manduka PRO** if you practise most days and the mat stays in one room. It is denser, longer, wider, and built to be the last mat you buy for a while.

If you are earlier than that decision, [how to choose a yoga mat](/guides/how-to-choose-a-yoga-mat) starts further back.

## Both say "lifetime guarantee". Only one covers wear.

This is the part almost every comparison skips, and it is the one that decides value.

**Gaiam's guarantee covers manufacturing defects only.** Its own page says the guarantee "covers manufacturing defects only (including any defect in materials or workmanship)" and that "normal wear and tear is not covered under this Lifetime Guarantee". It applies to the original purchaser, to US residents only, and the sole remedy is a replacement mat.

**Manduka's guarantee covers the opposite thing.** Its page covers "wear to the top surface of the yoga mat from regular use that impacts its performance" — the wear a mat actually gets. Cosmetic marks that do not affect performance are excluded.

So when a Gaiam mat goes slick and compressed after a couple of years, that is normal wear, and the guarantee does not apply. When a PRO's surface wears to the point it stops performing, that is exactly what Manduka says it will replace.

Read the Manduka exclusions before you buy, though, because they are strict:

- Damage from excessive water, including submerging the mat
- Damage from a washing machine or a vacuum cleaner
- Cleaning with anything other than Manduka Mat Wash or water
- Creases from improper storage
- Mats used by a studio as in-studio equipment

Proof of purchase is required, and the replacement is limited to once per purchase. If you have been cleaning your mat in the bath, our guide on [how to clean a yoga mat](/guides/how-to-clean-a-yoga-mat) covers what to do instead.

## Weight is the difference you feel first

3.3 lb against 7.5 lb is not a spec-sheet detail. It is more than double.

The Gaiam is the mat you sling under one arm without thinking. The PRO is the mat you plan a trip around. OutdoorGearLab lists "hefty" as a con of the PRO and calls the Gaiam light enough to be a beginner's carry mat.

That weight buys something, though. A dense heavy mat lies flat, does not bunch, and stays put when you step back into a lunge. Our PRO review scores its stability 5, the highest mark we give any mat. Our Gaiam review scores stability 3, and notes the soft 6mm cushion feels a little vague in standing balances.

Two mats, same stated thickness, opposite feel. Density is doing that, not millimetres.

## Length matters more than most people expect

The Gaiam Premium is 68 inches long. The PRO is 71.

Three inches sounds like nothing until you lie down. At 68 inches, anyone around 5'10" or taller will find hands or feet drifting onto the floor in savasana and long poses. The 71-to-72-inch range is the standard almost everything else uses, so here the Gaiam is the short one, not the PRO the long one.

The PRO is also 26 inches wide against the Gaiam's 24. That is real room in a wide-legged forward fold, and it is why the PRO reads as a bigger mat in a room even though both are 6mm.

## Grip: neither of these is a sweat mat

If you sweat, this whole comparison is the wrong one.

OutdoorGearLab lists "poor wet grip" as a con of the Gaiam and "more slick when wet" as a con of the PRO. Our own scores agree and put numbers on it: we score the PRO's wet grip 3, and the Gaiam's 2. Neither is good.

They fail differently, which is worth knowing:

- The **Gaiam's** foam surface gets slippery as moisture builds, in OutdoorGearLab's words. There is no break-in that fixes it.
- The **PRO** starts slick on purpose. Manduka says plainly that the PRO's "grip improves over time as you practice". You have to use it for weeks before it grips well dry, and it is still the weaker choice in a heated room.

Both are closed-cell. Manduka describes that as non-porous, so "sweat, dirt, and bacteria stay on the surface of the mat instead of soaking in". Good for hygiene, bad for wet grip. Same trade in both mats.

For heated practice, look at a rubber or PU mat instead — the alternatives at the end of this post are the two we have checked links for.

## How long each one lasts

This is where the price gap earns or loses its keep.

Manduka states that "the lifetime of the mat is approximately 10 years of regular use when loved and cared for". That is the company's own estimate, not independent testing. OutdoorGearLab's tester reports an owner still using a PRO after 20 years, which is a single anecdote rather than a lab result, but it points the same way.

For the Gaiam, OutdoorGearLab is direct: it "compresses over time", is "less durable", and the material "can show signs of compression, scuffs, and wear with regular use". Our own review scores its durability 3 and frames the honest limit as a performance ceiling rather than a failure — the mat does not fall apart, it just goes soft and slick before a premium mat would.

So the sums depend entirely on how often you practise. A once-a-week practice may never reach the Gaiam's ceiling. Five sessions a week will.

## Materials: both PVC, different claims

Neither of these is a natural mat. Both are PVC.

Gaiam's page lists the Premium as PVC and latex-free, and Gaiam markets its mats as 6P free, meaning free of DEHP, DBP, BBP, DINP, DIDP and DNOP — the six phthalates most often flagged in cheap mats.

Manduka's claim goes further on manufacturing. It says that "while made from PVC, PRO mats are manufactured in Germany to be toxin-free, phthalate-free, and emissions-free". Emissions-free refers to how the mat is made, not to what it is made of.

Both claims are the manufacturers' own. If PVC itself is the thing you are trying to avoid, neither mat solves it, and you want the rubber options below.

## Who should buy neither

Three cases where this comparison sends you somewhere else entirely:

- **You sweat heavily or practise hot.** Both mats have weak wet grip. A rubber or PU surface is the fix, not a bigger budget.
- **You want natural materials.** Both are PVC, however clean the manufacturing claim. Rubber is the alternative.
- **You are tall and on a budget.** The Gaiam's 68 inches will annoy you every session, and the PRO's price may be out of range. A standard 71-inch budget mat beats both for you.

## Which should you buy?

If money is the deciding factor, or you are still building the habit, take the Gaiam. It is enough mat to learn on, and OutdoorGearLab's Best Buy award for a first mat is a fair summary of it.

If you already practise most days, take the PRO. You are paying for density, size and a guarantee that covers wear, and those only pay off across years.
