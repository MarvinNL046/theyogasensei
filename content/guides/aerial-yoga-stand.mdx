Every aerial stand on the market advertises a weight capacity. Max 310 lb. Max 440.9 lb. Max 551.2 lb. They are printed like specifications, and they are almost useless, because not one of those listings tells you the height the figure was measured at.

It turns out that matters more than anything else about the purchase.

## The number that changes when you raise the frame

One manufacturer publishes the honest version. PRIOR FITNESS states an SGS-verified **breaking load of 3,036 lb with the frame at 7.8 ft, and 1,608 lb with the same frame at 11 ft**.

Same rig, same steel. Extend it and its strength roughly halves.

That is intuitive once you see it — a taller A-frame is a longer lever with a shallower angle — and it makes every unqualified capacity claim in this category unreadable. A stand advertised at 551 lb might mean 551 lb at its lowest setting. The listing does not say, so you cannot know.

Two things follow. If you plan to run the rig near its full height, treat the advertised figure as optimistic. And when a brand publishes load *by height*, that is worth paying for on its own — not because the steel is better, but because you can actually reason about it.

Worth adding, because it is the same trap as the ceiling hardware: a **breaking load** is not a working load. [Uplift Active](https://upliftactive.com/products/ceiling-x-mount-hook-set-of-2) rates its aerial ceiling hooks at 330 lb working load with a 10:1 safety factor — a far more conservative kind of number than 3,036 lb of breaking strength. Do not compare the two directly.

## Height is set by your fabric, not your preference

The second mistake is buying a stand by how tall the room is.

Your hammock decides the height. Aum Active publishes 8 to 11 feet as the recommended hanging height for its 5.5-yard hammock, and sells a 4.5-yard version for lower setups. Buy a rig that lands inside your fabric's range, or buy an adjustable one and set it there.

This catches people who already own the fabric. The compact stand we recommend on the [aerial hammock guide](/guides/aerial-yoga-hammock) stands 7 ft 10 in — just under the 8 ft that a 5.5-yard hammock is written for. That pairing works if you shorten the fabric, and it is exactly the kind of mismatch nobody flags at checkout.

Measure the **usable height under the top bar**, not the overall height. The difference is the depth of the bar and the hardware hanging from it, and it is several inches.

## Height costs floor, and not proportionally

This is the number that decides whether the rig fits your house.

Comparing two frames from the same brand, where the published dimensions are directly comparable:

| | Compact frame | Tall frame |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Height | 7 ft 10 in | 9 ft 8 in |
| Footprint | 67 × 51 in | 100 × 76 in |
| Floor area | **~24 sq ft** | **~52 sq ft** |
| Frame weight | 38 lb | 50 lb |

A 23% gain in height takes **more than twice the floor**. The legs have to splay further to stay stable, so the base grows in both directions at once.

Measure the room before you fall in love with a height. And note what is missing from that table: two of the most-sold stands in this category publish no dimensions at all. If a listing will not tell you its footprint, assume the larger one.

## What we would do

Measure the floor first, then the fabric, then buy the shortest rig that clears your hanging height. Height is the expensive dimension in every sense — it costs floor, it costs money, and it costs capacity.

If you are buying near the top of a frame's range, buy the one that publishes load by height. It is the only way to know what you actually have.
