If you are between 5ft 6in and 5ft 11in, buy the **65cm** ball. Every seller checked here agrees on that band, and it is the size they all call their most popular.

Step outside it and the agreement falls apart.

Five sellers publish five different height charts. At 5ft 2in, three of them put you on a 55cm ball and a fourth puts you on a 65cm. That is a whole size apart, on the same product, from companies selling to the same person.

So a chart is a starting point, not an answer. Here is what they say, why they disagree, and the one check that settles it.

## Why the charts cannot agree

Because they are all predicting the same thing indirectly: where your knees end up when you sit down.

Height is a rough stand-in for leg length, and a poor one. Two people at 5ft 7in with different inseams sit at different heights on the same ball. The charts are guessing, and they guess differently.

Retrospec says this out loud in a way the others do not. Its guide notes that small changes in firmness shift how well a ball matches your height. Over-inflate it and it feels tight and bouncy. Under-inflate it and it goes squishy and uneven.

Which brings us to the number that actually decides this.

## The centimetre on the box is not the ball you own yet

**The size printed on the box is the diameter when the ball is fully inflated.** Gaiam states it plainly: the diameter of an exercise ball is the same as its height at full inflation.

Your new ball is not at that diameter. It gets there over a day or two, and only if you inflate it twice.

All three sellers publish a version of the routine, and no two are the same:

1. **Gaiam** — inflate, let it stand 24 hours, deflate it by half, then refill to full height. Its listing warns you may need to repeat this once or twice.
2. **URBNFit** — inflate to about 80%, let it stretch overnight, then top it off.
3. **Trideer** — inflate to 80%, rest 24 hours, then inflate fully, and check the diameter with a tape measure.

They differ in the details. They agree on the point: the material stretches, and one pass of the pump does not give you the ball you paid for.

This is the whole failure. Somebody follows a height chart, pumps the ball once, sits on it, finds their hips too low, and decides the chart sent them the wrong size. The chart was fine. The ball was two inches short of itself.

Trideer adds one more figure worth keeping. A correctly inflated ball should compress about two to three inches when you sit on it. So "correct" moves twice: once as the material stretches, and again the moment you put weight on it.

## The check that beats every chart

Retrospec publishes the test, and it takes ten seconds.

1. Inflate the ball fully, using the two-stage routine above.
2. Sit on it with both feet flat on the floor.
3. Your knees should be level with your hips, or just below them.
4. Your feet should rest flat, without stretching down or curling under.

If your hips sit lower than your knees, the ball is too small — or, far more often, not yet at full size.

Sizing for a desk is a separate question, and the desk wins. Trideer scopes its 55cm to shorter adults and desk work, but the ball has to put your hands at your keyboard. Measure the desk before you measure yourself.

## The second number nobody explains

Here is the load figure on a set of comparable balls, as each seller publishes it:

| Seller | Published figure | How it is labelled |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gaiam (own site) | 300 lb | body weight the ball suits |
| URBNFit | 330 lb | "load" |
| SISSEL Securemax (via Balanced Body) | 330 lb / 150 kg | weight capacity |
| ElaraFit | 550 lb | "capacity" |
| APEXUP | 660 lb | unlabelled |
| ProBody | 880 lb | "load" |
| KEEP | 1,100 lb | "heavy duty anti-burst" |
| Black Mountain | 2,000 lb | **static** strength, independently tested |
| Trideer (own guide) | 2,200 lb | anti-burst tested to |

Three hundred pounds to two thousand two hundred, on the same shelf, for the same object. These are not the same measurement.

**Only Black Mountain says the word "static".** A static load is a dead weight resting on a stationary ball. It is not a person bouncing, kneeling, rolling into a plank or standing up in a hurry. It sits closer to a breaking figure than to a working one.

Notice which sellers publish the small numbers. Gaiam says its balls suit a body weight up to 300 lb. Balanced Body lists the SISSEL Securemax at 330 lb. Those are the two most conservative figures in the table, and they are the two that read like they are describing a person.

Our reading: the four-digit numbers are marketing, the three-digit numbers are engineering, and a listing that gives you a big number without the word "static" has told you nothing. Do not rank on it.

"Anti-burst" gets the same treatment. Wikipedia traces the family to a 1963 process for moulding large puncture-resistant plastic balls, and every listing here uses the phrase. Exactly one of them says what it means physically: URBNFit publishes a 1.7mm wall, thick enough to leak slowly rather than blow out. The rest publish the badge and no test.

## What we would do

Take the 65cm unless you are clearly outside 5ft 6in to 5ft 11in, and treat the charts above and below that band as a suggestion.

Then spend two days on the inflation. Pump to roughly 80%, plug it, leave it overnight, and top it up the next day. Measure it against a wall mark if you want to be sure. Gaiam's own method is to mark the wall at 55, 65 or 75cm and inflate until the top of the ball reaches the mark.

Then sit on it. Knees level with hips or just below, feet flat. If that is wrong after the ball has finished stretching, then and only then did you buy the wrong size.
