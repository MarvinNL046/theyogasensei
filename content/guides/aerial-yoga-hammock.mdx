Buying an aerial yoga hammock is two decisions, and every listing on the first page of results only helps with the easy one.

The fabric is the easy one. The hard one is what holds it up — and the kits do not include that part, do not tell you what it costs, and in two cases claim their fabric can take over a tonne while the anchor you will actually hang it from is rated for a fraction of that.

Start with the second decision. It is the one that can hurt you.

## The short answer

If you rent, or nobody qualified has looked at your ceiling, **buy a freestanding rig** and hang the fabric from that. No drilling, no structural assessment, and you can take it outside.

If you have a suitable anchor already installed by someone qualified, **a 5.5-yard tricot hammock with hardware in the box** suits a ceiling of roughly 8 to 11 feet. Below that, buy the 4.5-yard version instead.

## The number on the box is not the number that matters

Every listing leads with a weight capacity. Here are four, as published:

| Kit | Claimed capacity |
| --- | --- |
| Aum Active | 500 lb fabric, "over 2000 lb" for the rigging accessories |
| VEVOR | 2,204.62 lb / 1,000 kg |
| Yes4All | 660 lb |
| Freestanding stand | 440.9 lb |

Four products, a fourfold spread, and not one names a testing standard. Read them as marketing, not as specification. They are also answering the wrong question.

The question is what your ceiling can hold, and there the published numbers get much smaller and much more careful. [Uplift Active](https://upliftactive.com/products/ceiling-x-mount-hook-set-of-2) — the shop ranking first for this search — sells aerial ceiling hooks with a working load limit of **330 lb at a 10:1 safety factor**. The same page says the hook must be installed by an aerial-experienced licensed contractor or professional rigger, that not all beams are safe for aerial, and that improper installation can result in injury or death.

That is a company selling you the part, telling you plainly not to fit it yourself.

The reason the safety factor is that large is movement. A hammock is not a shelf. Swinging and spinning load the anchor well beyond what you weigh, which is why rigging guidance for suspended people works to ratios like 5:1 or 10:1 rather than to bodyweight.

## The option that skips the ceiling entirely

For most people reading this at home, the honest recommendation is a freestanding frame. It removes the entire structural question, which is the only genuinely dangerous part of the decision.

The trade-offs are real and worth knowing before you order. A frame takes floor space a hammock does not. It has its own capacity, lower than any fabric claim. And its height is fixed, which matters more than it sounds: the rig below stands about 7 feet 10 inches tall, while the 5.5-yard hammock is written for 8-to-11-foot hanging heights. Pair them and you may be shortening the fabric to suit.

## Then choose the fabric

Once the hanging point is settled, this part is genuinely easier than the listings make it sound. Three things decide it.

**Length, matched to your ceiling.** Aum Active publishes 8 to 11 feet as the recommended hanging height for its pre-knotted 5.5-yard hammock and sells a 4.5-yard version for lower rooms. That is the most specific guidance any of these brands gives. Two other kits claim their length "fits all ceiling heights", which cannot be true of both a low basement and a studio.

**Stretch, and the listings disagree.** Aum Active describes "just the right stretch", VEVOR advertises "excellent elasticity", and Yes4All calls its fabric "non stretch" — in a category where low-stretch tricot is the norm for hammocks. If you want a predictable, firm feel, read the fabric description rather than the brand.

**What is in the box.** Most kits include carabiners, extension straps and o-slings. None includes the ceiling anchor. Aum Active is the only one of the four that says so outright: "Mounting rig is NOT INCLUDED."

## Before you buy anything: two questions for a doctor

Aerial puts you upside down, and that is not neutral for everyone.

The clearest evidence concerns the eyes. A [systematic review and meta-analysis of yoga and intraocular pressure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10391478/) found that head-down postures raise eye pressure within a minute of assuming them, with one case report recording a twofold increase during a headstand compared with sitting. Pressure returned to baseline within two minutes of coming upright, but the authors' recommendation is unambiguous: patients with glaucoma should be advised against practising head-down postures.

Beyond that, the conditions that come up repeatedly across aerial studios' own intake guidance are uncontrolled high blood pressure, heart conditions, vertigo, recent surgery, back or neck injury, and pregnancy. We are not a medical source and will not pretend to rule any of them in or out. If one applies to you, that is a conversation with a doctor before it is a purchase.

One more that applies to nearly everyone: **learn the entries with a teacher first.** This guide covers what to buy and will not tell you how to get into the fabric, because that is not something to read off a page while hanging in your living room.

## What we would actually do

Buy the stand, buy the 5.5-yard hammock, and check the two heights against each other before ordering. That combination costs more than a bare kit and removes the only part of this purchase that could genuinely go wrong.

If you already have a professionally installed point, skip the stand and buy the fabric that matches your hanging height.

And if neither is true yet, the honest sequence is: take a class, decide you like it, then solve the anchor before you buy any fabric at all.
