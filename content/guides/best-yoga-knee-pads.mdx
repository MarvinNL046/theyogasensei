Before anything else, two questions. Where exactly does your knee hurt, and do you own a blanket?

Those two answers decide whether you need to buy anything at all, and no page currently ranking for this product asks either of them. I checked — page one for this term contains no independent editorial roundup at all. Every result that is not Reddit is a brand or a retailer selling its own pad.

> **Quick answer.** Pain on the **front of the kneecap** when you kneel is a pressure problem, and foam genuinely fixes it. Pain at the **sides, underneath, or deep in the joint**, or pain that appears when you rotate, is a movement problem — padding hides it rather than fixing it. If it is the first kind, try a blanket folded to about an inch under your shins before you buy anything, because that matches the thickest pads on the market and is better placed than any of them.

## Where does it hurt? The question that decides everything

A knee pad protects one specific structure. The prepatellar bursa sits between your kneecap and the skin over it, and per NIH-hosted clinical reference, its thin walls make it vulnerable to inflammation from "repetitive trauma from frequent kneeling". That is exactly what tabletop, cat-cow, low lunge, camel and thunderbolt do to it.

The same source is unusually direct about the fix: use protective equipment, such as bulky knee pads, for those who cannot avoid kneeling. So this is not a product category invented by marketers. For front-of-kneecap pressure, padding addresses the cause.

But that mechanism only reaches the front of the joint. There is nothing a pad under your knee can do about the inside of it.

Yoga therapist Beth Spindler gives the cleanest diagnostic line I found anywhere on this topic: a sensation of pulling at the sides of the knee or under the kneecap "is reason to back away from a movement."

So:

| Where it hurts | What it is | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Front of the kneecap, under bodyweight | Compression on the bursa | Padding — genuinely |
| Sides of the knee, under the kneecap, deep inside | Alignment or load | Technique, and possibly a professional |
| On rotation, in pigeon or lotus | Torque through the joint | Backing out of the pose |

Yoga instructor Amber Burke names the poses where the knee is most at risk of torquing or hyperextension: pigeon, lotus, triangle, pyramid, dancer and warrior III. Buying a pad for pain in those is buying the wrong thing.

One caution that cuts both ways. Physical therapist Bill Reif points out that pressure pain on the kneecap is often **delayed** — you may not know during class that you overloaded it. That argues for padding by default in kneeling poses. It also means "it felt fine at the time" is not evidence that you were fine.

## When to stop reading buying guides and see someone

Nicholas Sgrignoli, a sports medicine physician at the Hospital for Special Surgery, sets the threshold: see a doctor for persistent knee pain that affects your function or your ability to exercise, pain you cannot manage, or swelling, buckling or collapsing.

And one that is not a padding decision at all. Up to one third of prepatellar bursitis cases present as **septic**. A kneecap that is hot, red and swollen is a same-day medical question, not a shopping one.

## The free fix, and why it is not a compromise

The category leader opens its own product page with the line "Tired of folding your mat or towel for an extra cushion to alleviate knee pain?" — which tells you exactly what its real competitor is.

Here is that competitor, specified more precisely than any commercial page manages. Yoga International instructs placing a folded blanket **about an inch thick under the shins**, with the kneecaps just off the edge of the blanket.

Read that again, because it is doing two things at once:

**It matches the thickest pads sold.** An inch is 25mm. The Kinesis pad is 25mm. The firm SukhaMat is an inch. Gaiam claims an inch. The blanket is not the budget option — it is the same thickness.

**It is better placed.** Padding the shins lifts the kneecaps clear of the floor entirely, which is what Reif advises: keep the patellae free from any ground contact. A pad you kneel *on* still puts weight through the kneecap, just more softly.

Reif's own caveat keeps this honest: for some people, padding only the shins tips more weight forward onto the kneecaps, in which case pad both. It is conditional advice, not a rule.

Two more free options a studio teacher gives: fold your mat over on itself mid-class to double it where you kneel, or change the pose — a high lunge instead of a low one removes the problem entirely.

The fair counterpoint, from a teacher rather than a seller: a purpose-built pad is generally thicker and more consistent than a doubled-over mat, and it holds its shape. A blanket has to be refolded. If yours slides or will not hold an inch, that is when the pad earns its money.

Our own [thickness guide](/guides/how-thick-should-a-yoga-mat-be) already recommends this setup — a stable 4-5mm mat plus a small knee pad or folded blanket — rather than buying a thicker mat. A very thick mat costs you stability in every standing pose to solve a problem that happens in about four.

## The thing the marketing does not tell you about foam

This one is worth knowing before you buy the thickest pad you can find.

In balance research, foam pads are placed on force platforms **specifically to induce instability** — that is their purpose in the experiment. The peer-reviewed posturography work found that the pads of highest stiffness produced the largest change in sway.

You can see the same logic in the catalogue. ProsourceFit sells its NBR foam as a yoga knee pad, and sells a foam balance pad whose own description promises to improve balance and stability *through* instability. Same material family, opposite marketing.

ProsourceFit is at least explicit about the trade-off on the knee pad, saying its 15mm density is calibrated to give comfort "without being too thick to maintain stability". Kinesis makes the same argument for its 25mm pad — about the thickness of a deck of cards — on the grounds that it cushions without adding bulk to the whole mat.

The practical read: this matters for half-kneeling and lunges where you are balancing, and barely at all for tabletop or child's pose where you are not. Do not buy 25mm for a practice that is mostly standing.

Five pads, all with specifications I verified against both the manufacturer and the live listing. No scores, because I have not tested them.

### The default — ProsourceFit Extra Thick

24 by 10 inches, 15mm of high-density NBR foam, latex-free and phthalate-free per the brand. Big enough to cover both knees or knees and shins, and the brand also positions it for elbows, wrists, sit bones and head support.

It is the pick whose numbers agree across every source, which after auditing this category counts for more than it should. Care is simple: wipe with a damp cloth, air dry fully, not machine washable.

The known complaint, from a named yoga teacher rather than a marketplace: these flatten and indent with heavy use.

### What about cork?

Cork knee pads exist and I am not recommending one. The leading cork pad is 6mm thick — roughly a quarter of what the rest of the category uses. Cork's genuine advantage is that it grips *more* when wet, which is the opposite of most surfaces and genuinely useful. But grip is not the job here. Cushion is.

## Who should not buy one

- **Anyone whose pain is at the sides, underneath, or deep in the knee.** Padding cannot reach it, and buying one delays the fix.
- **Anyone who owns a blanket, a bath towel or a spare mat.** Test the free version this week. It costs you nothing to find out.
- **Anyone practising mostly standing sequences.** You are buying a solution for four poses you rarely hold.
- **Anyone with bony or tender knees looking at the extra-firm model.** That one is built for load, not for sensitivity.
- **Anyone with a hot, red, swollen kneecap.** That is a doctor, today.

## Read the listing sceptically

This category has unusually sloppy product data, and it is worth knowing how to check.

The number one brand contradicts itself on thickness in its own spec line. Gaiam contradicts itself three separate ways — dimensions, weight and material — across its site and its listing. One leading cork pad states a length in its spec table that disagrees with its own description. Two of the five pads here have weights that differ depending on which field you read.

None of that makes them bad pads. It does mean that if a figure matters to your decision, check it against the manufacturer's own page rather than the marketplace bullet.

## A note on scale

The research literature on kneeling injury studies floor layers, who spend 40 to 50 percent of the working day on their knees. A yoga class involves a few minutes.

That is worth saying plainly, because it sets the stakes correctly. For most practitioners this is a comfort purchase that lets you stay in a pose long enough to breathe in it — not an injury-prevention necessity. If it makes you practise more often, it has done its job.
