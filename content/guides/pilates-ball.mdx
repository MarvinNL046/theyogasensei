Search for a Pilates ball and Google will hand you a sizing rule within about two seconds. Under 5ft 4in, take the 55cm. Up to 5ft 11in, the 65cm. Taller than that, the 75cm.

That rule is correct. It is also for a completely different product.

Every ball on this page is between 7 and 9 inches across. The chart describes the large stability ball you sit on, which is roughly three times the diameter. Nothing about your height decides which small ball you want.

## The size question, answered properly

The small ball is picked by **where it goes**, not by how tall you are.

[Complete Pilates](https://complete-pilates.co.uk/pilates-exercise-balls/), a UK clinic, publishes both rules on one page and keeps them apart. The 55/65/75cm chart is scoped to the stability ball. For the small ball — they call it an over ball — the recommendation is 7 inches.

Here is the practical version, using the diameters these four brands actually state:

| Diameter | Where it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7.5 in | Between knees or ankles, where a bigger ball slips out | Trideer |
| 8 in | A middle size, comfortable in the hands | Bala |
| 9 in | Under the low back, between the palms, the clinic default | THERABAND, ProBody |

Two inches sounds like nothing. On a ball you hold between your knees it is the difference between a squeeze you can hold and one that keeps escaping.

## Firmness is a setting, not a spec

This is the part the listings bury, and it is the single most useful thing to know before you buy.

These balls all ship flat. **You** decide how firm yours ends up. That is not a defect of the cheap ones — it is how the category works, and two of the four brands write instructions around it.

What makes it interesting is that the sources contradict each other about which direction is "support".

[Bala](https://shopbala.com/products/bala-ball) tells you to over-inflate the ball for more support, or to half fill it to work harder. Complete Pilates says the opposite: a partially deflated ball gives greater comfort and support, while a fully inflated one adds instability and challenge.

Both are right, and the disagreement is the useful bit. They are describing different placements. Lying with the ball under your lower back, a softer ball is more comfortable and supports more of you. Squeezing one between your knees, a firmer ball gives you more to press into. "Support" means two different things depending on which side of the ball you are on.

So the spec that matters is not the diameter printed on the box. It is **whether you can change your mind afterwards**.

Three of these four seal with a plug: you inflate, you plug, and adjusting means starting over. Trideer is the only one with a needle valve, which lets you add or release air whenever you like. If I were buying one object to cover both placements, that is the feature I would pay for.

## The week nobody warns you about

Trideer's own listing says something none of the others do: there is roughly a one-week break-in period. After the first inflation the ball goes softer and needs topping up, because of how the PVC behaves. After that it settles.

Nothing is wrong with your ball. It is the material relaxing.

This matters more than it sounds, because it is the point at which most people decide their cheap ball is leaking and give up on it. It is also another argument for a valve — topping up through a plug means deflating and starting again.

## What "anti-burst" is worth here

Almost every listing in this category says anti-burst. It is worth roughly nothing as a way to tell them apart.

Look at ProBody's own comparison table and the anti-burst tick appears on all seven products in it — the 7-9 inch squeeze balls and the 45 to 85cm balls you put your body weight on, marked identically. THERABAND uses the phrase to describe a ball designed to stay elastic and squishy when fully inflated. None of these listings names a test standard behind the word.

The huge weight-capacity figures float around this category too, and they belong to the big ball, not this one. Nothing here is rated to be sat on, and a 3-ounce inflatable is not carrying your body weight in the first place.

We have run into this exact shape before: on [aerial gear](/guides/yoga-trapeze) a "holds up to 600 lbs" claim turned out not to be comparable to a stated working load limit. A number with no test behind it is not a specification.

Ignore the badge. Compare stated diameter, surface finish and how the thing seals.

## What we would do

Pick the diameter by where the ball will spend most of its time. Between the knees, go 7 to 7.5 inches. Under the back or between the hands, go 9.

Then buy one you can adjust, inflate it soft, and top it up after the first week once it settles. Do not chase the anti-burst badge, and do not read the height chart — it is not for you.
