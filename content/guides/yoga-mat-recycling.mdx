Your old yoga mat cannot go in the kerbside bin, and most of the recycling programmes the search results point you at will not take it either. That is the honest starting position, and nobody ranking for this question says it out loud.

I went through every route the top results name and tried each one. Five of the six were closed, in the wrong country, or not free. Here is what is left.

## The short answer

If your mat still grips and holds its shape, **give it away**. That is the only route that works everywhere, costs nothing and keeps the mat in use.

If it is genuinely finished — ripped, smooth, flaking — cut it up and use the pieces, then landfill what is left. A specialist recycler is worth chasing only if one operates where you live.

## Why the kerbside bin says no

Three reasons, and they stack.

**The material.** Most mats are PVC, which [Earth911](https://earth911.com/home-garden/recycling-mystery-yoga-mats/) calls one of the most difficult plastics to recycle. Its advice is blunt: even if your local centre takes PVC, it may still refuse yoga mats, so phone first.

**The shape.** The [EPA's recycling guidance](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/frequent-questions-recycling) says plastic bags, wraps and textiles do not belong in a kerbside bin. A yoga mat is a large flexible sheet with exactly that problem. [Hugger Mugger](https://www.huggermugger.com/blogs/yoga-gear/how-to-dispose-of-yoga-mat-safely-and-sustainably) puts the mechanism plainly: flexible sheets tangle in sorting machinery.

**The sweat.** The same source notes that a mat which has absorbed sweat and skin oils can contaminate the rest of the load. Yours has.

None of that changes if the mat is natural rubber. Hugger Mugger's point about landfill applies to the eco materials too: breaking down needs oxygen, moisture and microbes, and a sealed landfill supplies none of them quickly.

## The six routes people will send you to

I checked each one on 27 August 2026.

**Manduka via SuperCircle. It is over.** On paper this was the best offer in the category: send any mat, any brand, any condition, with a prepaid label, and take a discount off your next Manduka order. That is how it was announced in November 2024, and [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/manduka-launches-yoga-mat-recycling-program/) named the portal as manduka.supercircle.world.

Four checks, all pointing the same way. That address redirects to a Manduka blog post about sustainable design; so does manduka.com/pages/recycling, and neither page explains how to send a mat in. Manduka's own FAQ does not mention the programme. And [SuperCircle's own site](https://supercircle.world/) lists its partner brands — J.Crew, Reformation, Guess, Rothy's, Vans, Parachute, FIGS, Thousand Fell. Manduka is not among them.

Search results will still describe this programme in the present tense, because they are repeating the launch announcement rather than checking the page. It ran, and it stopped.

**ContinuOM Collective.** Real, and genuinely good: [Second Flow](https://continuom.org/) collects mats from every manufacturer, cleans and donates the usable ones, and recycles the rest into industrial products rather than burning or burying them. It has taken more than 6,000 PVC and 1,800 rubber mats. The catch for most readers is geography — the collection trial runs in Germany, the Benelux, France and Austria.

**LovEarth.** Also real, also somewhere else. It is based on Sydney's northern beaches, takes all brands and all types with no quantity limit, and charges a service fee for the sorting and rehoming work. It says it has repurposed over 6,000 mats in a decade.

**lululemon Like New.** Does not apply. The trade-in programme's [own help page](https://shop.lululemon.com/help/programs-and-discounts/like-new) excludes yoga props, and a mat is a prop. Clothing yes, mats no.

**JadeYoga.** There was a studio-collection programme years ago, and older articles still describe it. Jade's current FAQ has no take-back offer. Its answer to what to do with an old mat is a list of reuses and a pointer to freecycle.org, plus an email address.

**TerraCycle.** This one works, for money. The yoga-mat route is a purchased Zero Waste Box rather than a free sponsored programme, which Earth911 confirms and the search results reflect.

## Give it away before you try to recycle it

Donation is the route with no fee, no postage and no country restriction, and it keeps the mat doing its actual job.

The condition rule matters. Hugger Mugger draws the line at clean and structurally sound, and says a mat that is pilling or flaking is past donating. Yoga Journal's version: ripped, worn, no longer grippy means recycle, not donate. Wash it properly first — our guide to [cleaning a yoga mat](/guides/how-to-clean-a-yoga-mat) covers what not to soak.

Who takes them, in rough order of success: local studios for spare class mats, community centres and schools, animal shelters for cage lining, and neighbours through a local giveaway group. Ask before you turn up with it.

## Cut it up

For a mat that is finished, this is where nearly all of them actually end up, and it is not a consolation prize.

[Liforme's repurposing page](https://liforme.com/pages/repurpose-your-old-yoga-mat) lists more than ten uses and, notably for a mat brand, tells you to take PVC to a facility that accepts it. JadeYoga's FAQ answers the same question with its own list. CorePower Yoga's suggestion is the simplest: promote the old mat to outdoor mat and stop worrying about it.

The ideas that keep recurring across all three:

- Non-slip liners cut to size for drawers, shelves and the bottom of a toolbox
- A kneeling pad for gardening, cut long enough to shuffle along a bed
- Grip pads under rug corners and furniture feet so they stop travelling
- Bedding for a crate, or a washable layer under a pet bowl
- An outdoor or camping mat you never have to keep clean

## The part nobody selling mats wants to write

Recycling a yoga mat is mostly a story we tell ourselves. Of the six routes the top-ranking pages point a reader to, two have quietly ended, two only operate on other continents, one refuses mats outright, and the last one wants you to buy a box.

So the lever that actually moves anything is not disposal. It is not replacing the mat.

I own one mat — a Lululemon Reversible 5mm I have practised on almost daily since late 2024. At eighteen months the grip is unchanged and the damage is entirely cosmetic. Not because I looked after it especially well, but because sweat is what destroys the top surface, and most of mine landed on a towel.

## The cheap thing that prevents the problem

A grip towel sits between your hands and the mat and absorbs the sweat before the surface does. Then it goes in the wash, which the mat never can.

## Buying the next one with the end in mind

Two things are worth weighing before you replace anything, and both are covered properly elsewhere on this site.

First, whether the mat is actually finished. Our guide to [how long a yoga mat lasts](/guides/how-long-does-a-yoga-mat-last) sets out what genuine end-of-life looks like versus a mat that just looks tired.

Second, what "eco" means on a label. Most of it is marketing, and our [eco-friendly yoga mat guide](/guides/eco-friendly-yoga-mat) names the handful of brands where it is not. If disposal is the thing bothering you, that guide matters more than this one. And if you are starting from scratch, [how to choose a yoga mat](/guides/how-to-choose-a-yoga-mat) begins further back.
