Nearly every yoga trapeze is sold on the same promise. The listing for the product that named the category puts it as "instant back pain relief via traction and spinal decompression".

That is worth pausing on before you spend anything, because the research on inversion is considerably less certain than the marketing.

## What the evidence actually says

[WebMD's review of inversion therapy](https://www.webmd.com/back-pain/what-are-inversion-tables) reaches three conclusions, and only the first is good news for the seller. Some people do get short-term relief from low back or compressed-disc pain. Studies suggest it works no better than sham treatment. And it is probably not effective as a long-term treatment.

Short-term relief that does not beat a placebo is not nothing — plenty of comfort is placebo, and hanging genuinely feels good. But it is a different proposition from "instant back pain relief", and it should change what you expect for your money.

So here is the honest framing. Buy a trapeze because you want to hang, because supported backbends and shoulder opening appeal, and because inverting feels good to you. Do not buy it as a treatment.

## What it actually is

A trapeze is not an aerial hammock, despite the shared vocabulary and the overlapping search results.

A **hammock** is one loop of fabric you sit in and lie back over. A **trapeze** is a shorter sling with handles at several heights and ankle straps, built around hanging upside down. The hammock is closer to a restorative prop; the trapeze is closer to an inversion tool.

They also hang differently, and this is the practical difference at purchase. The trapeze is designed to go on a chin-up bar, a beam or a solid branch — the brand's own listing says so, and says in the same breath that ceiling hooks are not included.

## The rigging numbers do not mean what you think

The trapeze brand sells its own ceiling hooks, described as industrial strength and holding up to 600 lb. The aerial hooks we looked at on [the hammock guide](/guides/aerial-yoga-hammock) publish 330 lb — and that is the better number.

The difference is what the figure describes. A **working load limit** is what a fitting is rated to carry in use, with the safety factor already applied. [Uplift Active](https://upliftactive.com/products/ceiling-x-mount-hook-set-of-2) states 330 lb at a 10:1 factor, which implies a far higher failure point. "Holds up to 600 lbs" does not say whether any factor is included, or against what standard it was measured.

The same applies to the swing itself: "tested for 600 lbs" is the seller's own claim and names no standard.

None of this means the equipment is unsafe. It means the numbers on the box are not comparable to each other, and that the anchor above the swing deserves more of your attention than the swing does. If you are putting anything into a ceiling, that is a structural job — the aerial hardware sellers say themselves that it needs a licensed contractor or a professional rigger.

## Who should not buy one

This matters more here than on most gear pages, because inversion changes your physiology rather than just your comfort.

WebMD's page lists the conditions that should keep you off an inversion table: high blood pressure, glaucoma or other eye disease, heart disease, a history of stroke, hiatal hernia, inner-ear problems, pregnancy, and knee or hip arthritis. It notes that inverting slows the heartbeat, raises blood pressure and raises the pressure in your eyes, and it advises checking with a doctor first.

The eye finding is the one with the strongest evidence behind it. A [systematic review of yoga and intraocular pressure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10391478/) found head-down postures raise eye pressure within a minute of assuming them, and its authors advise glaucoma patients against practising them at all.

We are not a medical source and will not tell you whether any of this applies to you. If one of them might, that is a conversation before a purchase.
