Most people buy a yoga mat towel to stop sliding, and a good number of them are buying the wrong fix. A mat towel solves one specific problem very well. It does nothing for a second problem, and for a third it can make things measurably worse.

> **Quick answer.** If your mat goes slick once you sweat, a mat towel is the right purchase, and a budget one with grip dots does the job. If you slip with dry hands at the start of class, a towel is the wrong purchase — Manduka says so itself. If your mat grips fine and only your palms sweat, buy a hand towel instead and save most of the money.

By [Marvin Smit](/about) · Long-time practitioner, not a certified instructor.

I have not tested these towels. This page is built from what the manufacturers publish, what named testers at Wirecutter and OutdoorGearLab found, and the places those sources contradict each other — which turns out to be the most useful thing about the category, because nobody else prints it.

## First: which slipping problem do you actually have

Three different problems get sold the same product. Work out which one is yours before you spend anything.

**1. The towel or mat slides on the floor, or the towel slides on the mat.** This is a mechanical problem and it is what silicone nubs and silicone coatings are for. A towel with an anchored underside fixes it whether you are sweating or not.

**2. Your skin slides on the surface.** This is a moisture problem, and it is the one a towel is genuinely built for. Microfibre absorbs sweat that would otherwise sit on top of your mat as a film. Note the implication: it needs moisture to work.

**3. Your brand-new mat is slippery.** This is neither. New mats ship with a factory release film, and as Hugger Mugger explains, it wears off with normal use — a sea-salt scrub speeds it up. If your slipping started the day the mat arrived, you have a break-in issue, not a towel-shaped hole in your kit.

The reason this matters: buying a mat towel for problem 3 means paying for something that was going to fix itself. And buying one for problem 1 when your hands are dry can backfire, which brings us to the part most buying guides leave out.

## Who should not buy one

Two of the best sources on this category recommend against it for a large group of people, and one of them sells the towels.

Manduka's own hot yoga towel guide tells customers who slip with dry hands that a full mat towel is the wrong answer and that it "will only make you slip". Their suggested fixes are mat care, core strength and technique — none of which involve buying anything.

OutdoorGearLab's tester, several hundred hours into the category, is blunter still: for restorative yoga, outdoor practice in cooler temperatures, or any style where you simply do not sweat, a mat towel is "simply overkill".

Add three more cases where the honest answer is not a purchase:

- **You already own a grippy natural-rubber mat and only your palms sweat.** A hand towel covers it.
- **Your towel used to work and stopped.** That is almost always fabric softener, and the fix is a wash, not a replacement. See the care section below.
- **You are hoping to use a towel instead of a mat.** Wirecutter's testers found neither of the towels they tested had enough cushioning to be used alone. A towel is a surface, not a mat.

If you are still here, you sweat and your mat goes slick. A towel will help.

## Nubs, coating or nothing: what the makers actually say

The category divides into three underside treatments, and the marketing blurs them.

**Silicone nubs.** Small raised dots across the underside that grip the mat. Manduka's Yogitoes uses them and holds a patent on the design. A textiles expert at the Fashion Institute of Technology, speaking to Wirecutter, described the Yogitoes dots as 100% silicone and spaced further apart than a budget rival's.

**Silicone coating.** A continuous layer rather than discrete dots, used by Heathyoga among others.

**Nothing.** Manduka's eQua deliberately has no anchor at all — the brand calls it "a smooth, anchor-free design keeps your flow seamless".

Here is the trade-off, framed by the company that makes both. Manduka states that the un-nubbed eQua develops grip faster on first sweat but weakens once it is saturated and can start to move, while the nubbed Yogitoes absorbs more and holds on longer. That is a genuine practice-dependent choice, not a good-better-best ladder.

One caution on the dots. Not all grip dots are silicone. Eunzel publishes a comparison table on its own listings giving the dot material per model, and its mat towels are listed with PVC dots rather than silicone. That is a real, checkable difference nobody on the first page of Google mentions — and it is the kind of thing worth checking on any budget towel before you assume "grip dots" means one thing.

## Do you have to wet it? Mostly yes, and it is per-product

This is the most useful unpublished detail in the category. Three manufacturers say the same thing in different words, and two of them ship a spray bottle in the box to make sure you do it.

- **Manduka, nubbed Yogitoes:** lightly mist the **top** side before practice.
- **Manduka, smooth eQua:** lightly spray or dampen the towel before use — both sides.
- **Heathyoga:** the silicone coating is paired with "the included spray bottle for lightly misting".
- **IUGA:** "it is recommended to spray some water on the surface of the towel (A spray bottle is included)".

Hugger Mugger states the consequence plainly: if your hands and feet are dry at the start of class, the towel might actually feel slippery. That is the product working as designed and not yet activated — not a defect, and not something you should return.

Worth knowing that this is contested. Wirecutter tested two nubbed towels across several 90-minute hot classes and never mentions wetting them at all. My reading of the disagreement: the wetter your practice, the less the pre-mist matters, and the first ten minutes are where it matters most.

## Size it to your mat, not to the category

"Too narrow for my mat" is the most repeated complaint across the published roundups, and it is entirely avoidable.

Most mat towels are 24 inches wide, matching a standard mat: the Yogitoes is 71 by 24, the eQua mat towel 71 by 24. Some run wider — Heathyoga is 26 inches. If you practise on a wide mat, a 24-inch towel will leave rails of exposed mat down both sides. If you practise on a standard mat, a 26-inch towel will fold over the edges.

Measure your mat first. It takes ten seconds and it is the single most common regret in this category.

## Care: one mistake ruins a towel

If your towel stopped gripping, this is almost certainly why.

**Never use fabric softener or dryer sheets.** Manduka states that they "will make the surface of your product slippery". Hugger Mugger says the same independently: softeners leave a coating that makes the fibres slick. Microfibre works by having an enormous amount of surface area, and softener fills it in.

**The revival trick.** A towel that has stopped absorbing because of detergent or softener buildup can often be brought back with one detergent-free wash plus a cup of white vinegar in the rinse. That distinction — recoverable versus finished — is worth trying before you replace anything.

**Wash cold, dry low or hang.** Manduka's own routine is cold water, like colours, tumble dry low or hang dry, no bleach. Sources disagree on the dryer: Wirecutter reports both towels survived repeated machine washing and drying with no dots harmed, while microfibre manufacturers outside the yoga industry warn that high heat melts the fibres. Hang-drying costs nothing and sidesteps the argument.

**Never roll a damp towel inside your mat.** Manduka, Hugger Mugger and Yoga Journal each warn against it separately. It is the fastest route to mildew, and the smell does not wash out.

For mat-side care, our [guide to cleaning a yoga mat](/guides/how-to-clean-a-yoga-mat) covers the routines by material — the wrong cleaner shortens the life of a good mat the same way softener shortens the life of a good towel.

## Where the sources disagree

Three contradictions I could not resolve, published rather than smoothed over, because knowing they exist tells you how firm the "expert consensus" really is.

1. **Bunching.** OutdoorGearLab says the Yogitoes bunches underfoot when dry. Verywell Fit lists no bunching as one of its strengths. Same towel, two hands-on sources.
2. **Whether nubs matter.** Wirecutter found premium and budget nubbed towels equally functional. Shape, the highest-ranking magazine roundup for this term, never uses the word silicone once and picks un-nubbed towels for both its top slots.
3. **Wetting.** Manduka instructs misting even its nubbed flagship. Wirecutter tested nubbed towels through several hot classes and never raises it.

If a page tells you the category is settled, it has not read the other pages.

## How much do you actually sweat?

Worth a number, because the whole decision rests on it. In a study of hot yoga at 38.7°C, twenty-one participants showed a mean sweat rate of 0.9 litres per hour, with a spread of plus or minus 0.6. That is a wide range, and it is the reason two people can practise in the same room and reach opposite conclusions about the same towel.

If you are at the low end of that spread, you are closer to the "you may not need one" group than the marketing suggests.
